BUYING GUIDES

A Practical Pre-Purchase Inspection Guide for Classic Cars

What to check, what to ask for, and what to walk away from before you buy — a structured approach to inspecting a classic car in person.

Buying a classic car is rarely a purely rational transaction — there's usually some emotion involved, whether it's nostalgia, a long-held ambition, or simply falling for a particular car. That's fine. But a structured inspection, done before emotion takes over the decision, is what separates a good purchase from an expensive lesson.

Start with the paperwork

Before you even look at the car closely, ask for its documentation: title or registration, service history, previous ownership records, and any restoration invoices. A car with a complete, verifiable paper trail is worth more — and is far less likely to hide surprises — than one with a vague or incomplete history. Gaps aren't automatically disqualifying, but they're a reason to ask more questions, not fewer.

Check that the vehicle identification number (VIN or chassis number) on the paperwork matches the number stamped on the car itself, and that both match the engine number where applicable. A mismatch isn't always sinister, but it always deserves an explanation.

Bodywork and structure

Rust is the classic car buyer's most persistent adversary. Check the usual trouble spots: sills, wheel arches, floor pans, boot floor, and the base of the windscreen and rear window. Run a magnet (a fridge magnet works fine) over areas that feel or look like they might have filler — it won't stick to filler the way it sticks to steel. Look underneath the car, not just at the parts visible from the driver's seat, for evidence of accident repair, uneven panel gaps, or overspray suggesting a respray that wasn't declared.

Mechanical condition

A cold start tells you more than a warm one — ask to start the car from cold, and listen for excessive smoke, knocking, or reluctance to idle smoothly. Check fluid levels and condition: oil that looks like a milkshake can indicate a head gasket problem; coolant that's rusty or oily is worth investigating further. If you're not confident assessing mechanical condition yourself, this is the point where a marque specialist or independent inspector earns their fee many times over — particularly for higher-value cars.

Originality and matching numbers

For many collector cars, how much of the vehicle remains original — engine, gearbox, interior, trim — materially affects both value and desirability. It's one of the factors Collector Score grades directly. Ask directly what's been replaced, and request evidence for any "matching numbers" claim rather than taking it at face value. A car doesn't need to be 100% original to be a good purchase, but you should know what you're actually buying versus what's been represented to you.

The test drive

Drive the car for long enough to notice how it behaves once it's properly warmed up, not just around the block. Listen for unusual noises under acceleration, braking and cornering. Check that the car tracks straight under braking and doesn't pull to one side. If the seller won't allow a proper test drive, treat that as a meaningful red flag rather than a minor inconvenience.

Building the record from day one

Whatever you decide, keep everything you gather during the inspection — photographs, notes, the seller's answers to your questions, any specialist inspection report. If you do buy the car, that inspection record becomes the first entry in its ownership history with you, and it's exactly the kind of documentation that strengthens the car's provenance — and its eventual valuation — for whoever owns it after you.

The inspection you do before buying is the first page of the car's history under your ownership — worth keeping, not just acting on.
